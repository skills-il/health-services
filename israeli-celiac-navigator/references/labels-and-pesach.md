# Labels, cross-contamination and Pesach

## Israeli gluten labelling, and the format change

**The gluten regulation.** תקנות בריאות הציבור (מזון) (סימון גלוטן), התשנ"ו-1996,
as amended in 2011:

- food with gluten **above 20 parts per million** must show, in the ingredient
  list, the **name of the plant** that is the source of the gluten and the words
  **"מכיל גלוטן"**
- food may be marked **"ללא גלוטן"** only if its gluten is no more than 20 parts
  per million, it was produced under proper manufacturing conditions, and steps
  were taken to keep the raw materials and the food at that level
- the legal definition of gluten covers wheat, rye, barley and oats and their
  products, including starches, malt and bran

Source: https://www.gov.il/BlobFolder/legalinfo/health-mazon11a/he/files_legislation_food_health-mazon11A.pdf

**The EU allergen format (Regulation 1169/2011), being phased in.** From
**1 January 2028** manufacturers and importers must label allergens by
**emphasising them inside the ingredient list** (a different font, style or
background colour) instead of in a separate information box, and the new format
may be used already from 1 January 2025. The Ministry's guide to the new format
calls adding "מכיל גלוטן" right after the grain name, in the same size and emphasis,
a **recommendation**, while the 1996 regulation above still reads as requiring the
words. How the two interact is unsettled (the point was contested at the Knesset
Health Committee on 11.05.2026), so never rely on the words being present. The
guide itself says it is not legal advice and must be read with the binding
regulation. Spelt, durum and khorasan are wheat and must be named as wheat. A
product that meets EU Regulation 828/2014 (under 20 parts per million) can be
called gluten-free while still showing an oat ingredient emphasised in the list,
and should then not carry "מכיל גלוטן". So a "ללא גלוטן" pack can contain oats.
Whether this person may have oats yet is the treating team's call (the paediatric
guideline advises avoiding them at first), so the mark does not decide that.

**"ללא גלוטן" versus "אינו מכיל גלוטן".** Only "ללא גלוטן" is the regulated mark,
with the 20 PPM and manufacturing-conditions requirements. The Celiac Rights
Organisation also tells families to look for an explicit "אינו מכיל גלוטן", but the
regulation attaches no conditions to that wording; treat it as the manufacturer's
statement and confirm in writing where doubt remains. Allergen labelling of unpackaged (bulk)
food is deferred to **1 January 2032**, which is the legal reason the unpackaged
tier is a no.

**What a parent sees until 2028.** Two formats side by side: the grain
emphasised in the list (for example "קמח חיטה (גלוטן)"), or the old separate
"מכיל: גלוטן" line after the list. So teach the parent to read the ingredient
list for the grain itself, wheat (including spelt), barley, rye and oats, not to
scan only for the words "מכיל גלוטן" or for a box.

The Ministry's own words, verbatim:

- Reform deck (December 2024): "החל מ-1 בינואר 2028, יחויבו יצרנים ויבואנים לסמן אלרגנים במזון בצורה התואמת את הדרישות בתקנה. עם זאת, ניתן יהיה להתחיל בסימון החדש כבר מ-1 בינואר 2025."
- Allergen guide v2: "לתשומת הלב - לטובת חולי הצליאק, משרד הבריאות ממליץ להוסיף את המילים: "מכיל גלוטן" מיד לאחר שם הדגן המכיל אותו, באותו גודל ובאותו הדגש"

**The shelf method that follows.** Read the ingredient list for the grain itself
in whichever format the pack uses; prefer a "ללא גלוטן" mark (an oat ingredient still goes to the team, whatever the mark); treat "אינו מכיל
גלוטן" and a clean list as the manufacturer's word, not clearance; and **where
doubt remains, do not consume the product until it has been checked**.

**Spelt and buckwheat.** כוסמין is spelt, a wheat, and sold in Israel as the
"healthier" bread. כוסמת is buckwheat, not a wheat, but Leumit lists it among the
disputed grains with a contamination risk. One letter apart; never let an agent
merge them.

## Home kitchen

Prepare gluten-free food separately from gluten-containing food, on a completely
clean work surface, and prepare the gluten-free food FIRST and the
gluten-containing food last (school sandwiches are the everyday case). **Do not
use wooden utensils, spoons or boards that have been used with gluten-containing
food**, because wood holds it. The shared toaster is the other everyday route:
crumbs from gluten bread are in it, so Leumit advises a separate toaster. Store dry gluten-free foods on separate shelves and in separate
packaging from dry gluten-containing foods such as flours.

## Practical shopping method

- Read food labels.
- Where a product is unclear, clarify with the **manufacturer** that it does not
  contain gluten, and obtain the confirmation **in writing**.

## The grains

Gluten-containing: **wheat (including spelt), barley, rye**, and **oats**, the
last being the contested one. Leumit lists oat products (quaker, granola, oat matza, oat bran)
among those to avoid.

**Disputed by contamination, not composition**: buckwheat, millet, quinoa, teff,
amaranth and sorghum. They are probably safe in themselves, but there is a real concern about
contamination with gluten-containing grains **during harvest or milling**. This
is a supply-chain risk, not a property of the grain, which is why manufacturer
confirmation matters more than the botanical name.

## Pesach: two errors in opposite directions

**Error 1, the dangerous one: treating "kosher for Passover" as "gluten free".**
Not everything kosher for Passover is suitable for people with celiac. **Matza
flour** is the explicit counterexample.

**Error 2, the wasteful one: avoiding Passover products entirely.** Products
that are kosher for Passover and do **not** contain matza flour or Passover
flour **can be** suitable for celiac. This is why the shelves are unusually
friendly during the season. "No matza flour" rules a product in for the normal
label check; it does not replace it.

**Seder matza.** Oat matza contains oats (Leumit lists oat matza among the
products to avoid), so whether it is allowed yet is the treating team's call,
especially soon after diagnosis. Look for a matza marked "ללא גלוטן" and read
its ingredient list, because a gluten-free-marked matza can itself be made from
oats, and then the oats question still applies. The halachic question goes to
the family's rabbi.

**The vocabulary trap.** Kitniyot is a **kashrut** category, not a gluten
category:

| | Kashrut concept | Gluten status |
|---|---|---|
| Chametz | Leavened grain, a kashrut category | Overlaps with gluten grains but is not the same test |
| Kitniyot | Legumes and rice, permitted or not by custom | Says nothing at all about gluten |
| Matza flour | Kosher for Passover | **Contains gluten** |

A kitniyot-free product may still contain gluten. A kitniyot product may be
perfectly gluten free. Never let an agent merge the two vocabularies.

**The rule to give a family:** at Pesach, read specifically for matza flour and
Passover flour, and do not treat the holiday label as a safety certificate.
