# Israeli Celiac Navigator

## Legal notice

This is a free information tool operated by an AI model. It explains the diagnosis sequence, the
Israeli entitlements for celiac patients and how to read a food label, and it drafts requests you
send yourself. All of its output is produced automatically, with no involvement, review or approval
by a physician, a paediatric gastroenterologist or a registered dietitian, and an AI model may err,
omit information or present a wrong conclusion. **It is not a medical diagnosis, not a clinical
opinion and not dietary treatment.** It is background information and administrative help only. It
does not examine the patient, does not read serology or biopsy results, does not know the person's
other conditions or medications, and does not decide whether anyone should eat or avoid gluten. Do
not use it to diagnose or rule out celiac, to interpret a test result, to start or stop a
gluten-free diet, or to decide that a particular food is safe for a particular person; any request
text it drafts is an automatic draft for your own use. This tool is not a substitute for advice
that takes account of the particular circumstances and needs of each person, and all use of its
output is the user's sole responsibility.

## Critical Disclaimer

**Read this before using the skill. Every response based on this skill must repeat the relevant disclaimer.**

1. **This is a rights and logistics navigator, not medical advice.** Never diagnose celiac, never interpret a serology result, never tell anyone to start or stop a gluten-free diet as a medical decision. Diagnosis belongs to a gastroenterologist, and for children a paediatric gastroenterologist.
2. **The one thing to say early and loudly: do not remove gluten before testing.** If a user says they have already started a gluten-free diet and now want to be tested, tell them to raise it with the doctor before testing, because it changes what the tests can show. Do not tell them to resume eating gluten on their own; that is a medical instruction and it belongs to their physician.
3. **Celiac is not a named criterion on the Bituach Leumi disabled-child list** (allergy is). Never imply an allowance by analogy.
4. **Municipal welfare funding is budget-dependent.** Never promise an outcome; quote the ceiling (Step 7) only as a maximum before co-payment.
5. **Quote the IDF stipend only with its source date** (Step 6), never as today's rate, and never invent one.

## Problem

Celiac affects about one percent of the population (an Israeli study found at least 1:157), and diagnosis is where families make the mistake that is costliest to undo: removing gluten before testing, which can make the tests mislead. After diagnosis the entitlements are real but scattered (school substitutes and marked portions, a suitable medication alternative, municipal food funding, the army's food stipend), and the shelves fill with kosher-for-Passover products that are not gluten free. This skill puts the sequence in order and turns the entitlements into requests.

### Step 0.5: What actually makes people suspect it

Celiac rarely presents the way families expect. The recognised presentations are wide: anaemia, delayed sexual development, abdominal pain, vomiting, diarrhoea or
constipation, mouth ulcers, dental enamel defects, joint inflammation, an association with other
autoimmune conditions including diabetes, and behavioural presentations including depression,
hyperactivity and declining performance at school.

**A disturbance in liver enzymes can be the ONLY presentation.** Say it out loud: a family handed an abnormal liver panel is not thinking about gluten.

Celiac is no longer a childhood-only diagnosis; adults are diagnosed too. None of this is a diagnosis or a reason to change a diet; it is a reason to raise celiac with a physician while still eating gluten.

### Step 1: The diagnosis pathway, and the mistake to prevent

**Say this before anything else about diet:** a gluten-free diet started before the disease is
diagnosed by biopsy of the duodenal lining **may mislead and make diagnosis more difficult**. In the
standard pathway the child continues to eat gluten in the run-up to testing rather than stopping.

**Do not turn that into a protocol.** How much gluten, for how long, and whether the child is well enough to continue are the treating physician's decisions. If asked "for how long", the answer is that the doctor sets it; handing a family a duration to run on their own child is a medical instruction.

If the family has **already** gone gluten-free, do not improvise. Tell them to say so explicitly to the doctor at the referral, because it affects how the tests are interpreted and what the doctor may ask them to do next. Deciding whether and how to reintroduce gluten is a medical decision.

**What is actually tested.** The most sensitive antibodies for celiac are of the **IgA** group: antibodies against **TTG** and antibodies against **endomysium**. Because these antibodies are IgA, a state of **IgA deficiency**, which is more common in celiac patients, affects the serology. This is why total IgA is checked alongside, and why a family should not read a single negative number as a definitive all-clear. Where IgA is low, the Israeli paediatric guideline says celiac serology of the **IgG** type is done instead, so low IgA does not mean "the test does not work for my child".

**Biopsy.** Diagnosis normally rests on a biopsy with appropriate findings. The Israeli paediatric guideline (built on the 2012 European criteria) also describes an **exceptional** route without biopsy, which is a decision for the treating gastroenterologist under the guideline's criteria, not something a family can check against a lab result; it says asymptomatic children always need a biopsy, and that a gluten-free diet must not start before the diagnosis is complete. Which criteria apply today is the gastroenterologist's call. So a family told "no endoscopy needed" **by the gastroenterologist** has not been short-changed. If anyone else (a GP, a family member, a website) suggests starting the diet on a blood test alone, the move is to ask for the gastroenterology referral first, still eating gluten. On a **further** biopsy, Schneider's guidance lists an uncertain first diagnosis, or a diagnosis made **before age two**; whether one is needed is decided by the treating gastroenterologist. Worth raising for toddlers diagnosed very early, whose parents often assume the question is closed forever.

**Prevalence.** Celiac reaches about one percent of the population in adults and children. An Israeli study found a prevalence of at least **1:157**, and apparently that is an underestimate. Use this when a family feels like an anomaly; they are not.

### Step 2: What celiac is not

Agents blur this boundary constantly. Celiac is an autoimmune condition managed by lifelong dietary exclusion. It is **not** an IgE food allergy. There is no EpiPen, no anaphylaxis pathway, and no adrenaline. A user describing throat swelling and breathing difficulty after eating is describing something else entirely; route them to `israeli-food-allergy-navigator` and, if it is happening now, to adrenaline and 101.

The gluten-containing grains to avoid are wheat, barley, rye and **spelt (כוסמין)**, and oats, the last being the contested one. Spelt is wheat, and it is sold in Israel as the "healthier" bread. Do not confuse **כוסמין** (spelt, wheat) with **כוסמת** (buckwheat, not a wheat); the words differ by one letter. On oats, the Israeli paediatric guideline recommends complete avoidance in the initial period after diagnosis until clinical and serological recovery, and only then an optional trial under close monitoring, which is the treating team's call. Products such as buckwheat, millet, quinoa, teff, amaranth and sorghum are disputed: they are probably safe in themselves, but there is a real concern about **contamination with gluten-containing grains during harvest or milling**.

### Step 2.5: After diagnosis, the clinical follow-up nobody routes them to

Every item here is a REFERRAL to ask for, never a schedule to follow. The treating team sets intervals and targets.

- **Ask the kupah for a dietitian.** The treatment for celiac is the diet, so a clinical dietitian
  is not an optional extra, it is the treatment being taught. A referral to a kupat cholim dietitian
  is the first thing to ask for after diagnosis, and families routinely leave the gastroenterologist
  without one. Terms differ by kupah: Maccabi, for example, lists gastro conditions among the
  diagnoses that qualify for dietitian sessions, needs a doctor's referral for the first visit, and
  leaves the number of sessions to the dietitian. Check your own kupah's page rather than assuming
  another kupah's terms.
- **Raise screening of first-degree relatives.** Celiac runs in families, and the Israeli paediatric
  guideline names first-degree relatives as an at-risk group. Whether and when to test
  siblings and parents is the physician's call, but the question belongs on the table at diagnosis
  and it is very often never asked.
- **Ask what follow-up looks like.** Repeat serology after the diet starts, growth and weight
  monitoring in a child, and screening for associated autoimmune conditions are all things the
  treating team decides on. The Israeli paediatric guideline describes routine follow-up at least
  once a year, more often early on; quote that only as the guideline's floor, never as a schedule. The skill's job is to make sure the family knows to ask what the plan
  is, not to invent one.

Asked for an interval, a target antibody level or a screening age, say those belong to the physician and dietitian.

### Step 3: Gan and school

The education entitlement is broader than most parents realise, and it is worth quoting when requesting it. Its instrument is the Ministry of Education Director-General standing instruction **0404 of 01.08.2023, "תלמידים החולים במחלת הצליאק"**; name it in the request, because it is the first thing a principal asks for.

| Obligation | What to request |
|---|---|
| Substitutes everywhere | Suitable substitutes for celiac students in **every educational activity**, inside the institution **or outside it**, that involves **food or craft materials containing gluten**, so trips, food sales, birthdays and mishloach manot are covered too |
| Gluten-free offering | Wherever food products are supplied or sold in the institution, an offering of gluten-free food for celiac students |
| Distinct packaging | Portions intended for celiac students packaged in a **different colour** and marked with **special prominent marking** |
| Long-day meals | Where the institution serves meals, including a daily meal on a long school day, a portion suited to the child |
| No exclusion | The child takes part in every activity; the institution must avoid discrimination or exclusion on medical grounds |
| Parent duty | Parents fill in the **parents' health declaration form** before the start of the year; a medical document is sometimes required |

The colour-and-marking requirement is the anti-mix-up mechanism most often skipped; ask for it explicitly. Draft the request in Hebrew, short and factual, and keep it separate from any allergy framing so the institution does not apply an anaphylaxis protocol that does not fit.

**Food is not the only gluten in a gan, and craft materials are inside the entitlement.** Wheat-flour
play dough, flour-and-water craft dough, dry pasta threading and flour-based art are routine in
early-years settings, and a child of that age puts their hands in their mouth. Name them in the
request, because a request that names only food leaves the dough table where it was. The Ministry of
Education's 2026 "שמים לב לצליאק" models for gan and school are worth attaching.

### Step 4: Labels, shopping and medication

**Labelling, and the format is changing.** The gluten-labelling regulations (תקנות בריאות הציבור (מזון) (סימון גלוטן), 1996, amended 2011) require food with more than 20 PPM of gluten to show **the name of the plant that is the source of the gluten** and the words **"מכיל גלוטן"** in the ingredient list. Israel has also adopted the EU allergen rule (Regulation 1169/2011): from **1 January 2028** allergens must be **emphasised inside the ingredient list** (bold, a different font or a background colour) instead of in a separate box, and manufacturers may use the new format already since 1 January 2025. The Ministry's guide to that format calls adding "מכיל גלוטן" right after the grain name a **recommendation**, while the 1996 regulation still reads as requiring the words; how the two interact is unsettled, and was contested at the Knesset Health Committee in May 2026. In practice, never rely on the words being there. So until 2028 two formats share the shelf: the grain emphasised in the list (for example "קמח חיטה (גלוטן)"), or a separate "מכיל: גלוטן" line after the list. **Teach the parent to read the ingredient list for the grain itself**, wheat (including spelt, durum and khorasan), barley, rye and oats, emphasised or not, rather than scanning only for the words "מכיל גלוטן" or for a box that a new-format pack no longer has.

**What "ללא גלוטן" actually means. It is a threshold, not zero.** Food containing **up to 20 PPM** of gluten is considered gluten-free and permitted to eat. **Give it with its reason, or it reads as permission**: food that may contain gluten above that level must not be consumed, because even minute quantities can damage the intestinal lining.

**The more important half: the absence of "מכיל גלוטן" is NOT clearance.** The Israeli Celiac
Rights Organisation says plainly that the Health Ministry regulations are not clear on this point.
A pack with no "מכיל גלוטן" tells you the recipe does not include gluten. It does not tell you the
product was made in clean conditions or that there is no secondary contamination. So the method is:

1. look for an explicit statement on the pack, not merely the absence of a warning. **Only "ללא גלוטן" is the regulated mark**: it requires no more than 20 PPM AND production under proper manufacturing conditions. Packs also say **"אינו מכיל גלוטן"**, which the Celiac Rights Organisation tells families to look for, but the regulation attaches no conditions to that wording, so treat it as the manufacturer's statement and, where doubt remains, confirm with the manufacturer in writing. **Oats**: a pack can lawfully be "ללא גלוטן" and still contain oats. Whether this person may have oats yet is the treating team's call (the guideline advises avoiding them at first), so an oat product goes to the dietitian question, not into the trolley on the strength of the mark
2. read the ingredient list for the gluten grains, and any **"מכיל גלוטן"** or **"עלול להכיל"** line, in whichever of the two formats the pack uses; a "עלול להכיל: גלוטן" line is exactly the doubt case in point 3
3. **where doubt remains, do not consume the product until it has been checked**

That third rule is the one to repeat to a parent standing in an aisle.

**The home kitchen** is the other place a correctly-shopped child gets glutened: separate preparation on a clean surface with the gluten-free food made first, no wooden utensils or boards used with gluten (wood holds it), a separate toaster, and separate storage from flours. Detail in `references/labels-and-pesach.md`. Where a product is unclear, clarify with the manufacturer and get the confirmation in writing.

**Medication is the blind spot.** Two separate points:

1. Check whether the drug contains **flour or starch originating from wheat**. Excipients are where gluten hides in a medicine cabinet.
2. When the drug treatment in the **health basket is not suitable** for celiac patients, there is a **right to receive a suitable alternative**. Raise it with the physician and the kupah rather than paying privately.

### Step 4b: Birthday parties and the packaged-snack shelf

The most frequent real-world parent question, and not meal planning: which packaged party snacks can go in the bag so the child's plate looks like everyone else's. Sort the shelf into four tiers rather than memorising products; the fourth decides most party questions.

| Tier | What it means | How to treat it |
|---|---|---|
| Naturally gluten-free base | Corn, potato or peanut based snacks with no flour in the recipe | Never a blanket clearance for the brand. The seasoning is where wheat enters, so check flavour by flavour, and never say "the whole range is fine" |
| Same brand, two versions | The brand sells both a regular and a marked gluten-free product side by side | Only the pack carrying **"ללא גלוטן"** is suitable. The regular one is not. **A "כשר לפסח" marking is not a substitute**, see Step 5 |
| Dedicated gluten-free brands | Ranges built gluten-free, such as Schär, widely stocked including Nitzat HaDuvdevan, Anis and the large chains | Still check the marking; no brand is a blanket clearance. Pair with tier one |
| **Unpackaged, anything at all** | Homemade cake, a platter someone brought, bulk-bin sweets, a bakery cake, a chocolate fountain | **No.** No pack means no ingredient list and no traceability, however confident the person offering it is |

**The traps.** Bisli is a wheat-flour snack: wheat flour is the leading ingredient at roughly 80 to 85 percent of the product. Regular beigele is wheat. The Celiac Rights Organisation names cakes, cookies, wafers, Pesek Zman and Kif Kif among the gluten items; the parents' community list (unverified) adds Click, Krembo, KitKat and Twix, ice-cream cones and soup nuts. Treat all of them as out unless the pack in hand says otherwise. The community list also says the regular Kinder Surprise egg and Kinder Joy are not the same product, which is the transferable rule: **same brand, different product**, so check the marking on the specific box.

**Cake, table and setting.** Send an individually-wrapped gluten-free cupcake for the candle moment, cut with a clean knife and served from its own wrapper. Serve the child first, from its own sealed pack onto its own plate, with no shared bowls, dips or tongs. A party inside the gan or school is not shopping but the Step 3 entitlement; at a private home there is no entitlement, so the parent supplies the food. The full party playbook (the home-baked "gluten-free" cake, the scripted answer to "a little bite will not hurt", keeping the wrapper) is in `references/birthday-snacks.md`.

**The rule that must accompany any list.** Read the ingredient list of the pack in hand, in either labelling format (Step 4), never a remembered product name. Manufacturers change recipes and production lines, which is why a circulated list ages badly. For anything beyond a one-off party purchase, point the family at the **Israeli Celiac Rights Organisation** (<https://celiacrights.org.il>). `references/birthday-snacks.md` carries a community shortlist of specific products; present it as a starting point for the aisle, never as certification, and repeat the read-the-pack rule whenever you quote from it.

### Step 5: Pesach, where intuition fails

Passover is the season families most often get wrong, in both directions.

- **Kosher for Passover does not mean gluten free.** Matza flour is the obvious counterexample and it is explicitly named as unsuitable.
- **But the holiday genuinely helps**: products that are kosher for Passover and do **not** contain matza flour or Passover flour can be suitable for celiac, which is why the shelves are unusually friendly at that time of year. "No matza flour" rules a product IN for the Step 4 check; it does not replace it.
- **Seder matza**: oat matza contains oats, so whether it is allowed yet is the treating team's call, especially soon after diagnosis; look for a matza marked "ללא גלוטן" AND read its ingredient list, because a gluten-free-marked matza can itself be made from oats, in which case the oats question still applies. The halachic question goes to the family's rabbi.
- Kitniyot is a **kashrut** category, not a gluten category. A kitniyot-free product may still contain gluten, and a kitniyot product may be perfectly gluten free. Never let the two vocabularies merge.

The practical rule to give a family: at Pesach, read for matza flour and Passover flour specifically, and do not treat the holiday label as a safety certificate.

### Step 6: The IDF track

Often the most anxious question for a teenager, and the answer is reassuring.

- **Enlistment is compulsory**, not voluntary as it once was. Celiac patients receive a **regular enlistment medical profile together with a celiac clause**; with no other limitations, per Kol Zchut, **profile 97 with a celiac clause**.
- **Closed, per the IDF's own celiac page** (dated 27.11.2022): the submarine flotilla and the fire formation (עוצבת האש, the paratroopers brigade and the commando), **except the David's Sling formation (עוצבת קלע דוד)**, because of their operational role. Kol Zchut (updated July 2025) also lists reconnaissance units as closed, apart from pilot, naval officer and naval combat roles. Give both and tell the teen to confirm his own target with the IDF; do not interpret the carve-out further.
- **Open**: the IDF page says manoeuvring combat roles were opened to celiacs, **on a volunteer basis only**, declared in a dedicated preferences questionnaire (שאלון העדפות); nobody with celiac is assigned to them against their will. Kol Zchut names open roles including **pilot, naval combatant (boys) and recruit-squad commander**. Naming them matters more to an anxious teenager than the exclusions do.
- **Food**: gluten-free meals and bread in the dining halls, gluten-free combat rations in the field, gluten-free meals on enlistment day, and in reserves an "adapted food" indication that carries the food and stipend entitlement, per the IDF page.
- **Food stipend**: paid to the soldier's bank account **in addition** to dining-hall provision. The IDF page of 27.11.2022 states **21.9 NIS a day on an open base and 34.9 NIS a day on a closed base**; Kol Zchut says the amount may change, and no newer published figure was found (checked 29.10.2026), so quote it with that date and tell the soldier to confirm the current rate with the unit's welfare NCO (משקית ת"ש).

### Step 7: Money, honestly

**Start by managing expectations.** Celiac is not on the Bituach Leumi disabled-child impairment list, which does name allergy, so do not encourage a claim by analogy. An additional condition is a separate question for the general Bituach Leumi route.

**Income tax: no celiac route either.** The section 45 credit points for a parent of a child with a disability follow, for a child under 18, entitlement to the disabled-child allowance (or one of a few named conditions such as blindness or special-education placement). Celiac alone does not open it. Say so plainly rather than leaving the question unanswered.

**What does exist is mostly municipal:**

- Celiac patients **up to retirement age** who are known to and treated by the social services department, and who purchase medicinal or dedicated food **on a physician's recommendation**, may be eligible for funding assistance. It is given through the social services department, **subject to budget limits and the priorities set in each local authority**.
- Celiac patients who have reached **retirement age** and need special nutrition on a physician's recommendation may likewise be eligible.
- **The published ceiling.** Kol Zchut gives the assistance as **up to 1,210 NIS a month (as of 2026)**, with an income-based co-payment that is never below 10 percent, so the actual aid is between 10 and 90 percent of that ceiling. A family unhappy with the decision can appeal to the Welfare Ministry's district appeals committee.

Both are discretionary and budget-bound. Frame them as worth applying for, never as guaranteed. The physician's recommendation is the precondition to obtain first.

**A national item, reported but thin.** The Health Ministry subsidises a gluten-free flour. At the Knesset Health Committee on 11.05.2026 it was reported that about 1.2 million NIS a year is allocated and only about 300,000 NIS is used, and the Ministry acknowledged availability gaps in the retail chains. Mention it as worth asking for at the supermarket; do not quote a price.

## Examples

### Example 1: Parent suspects celiac and has already cut out bread

Removing gluten before diagnosis can mislead the tests. Tell them to disclose the change at the referral, never to resume gluten on their own, then outline what testing involves.

### Example 2: Newly diagnosed 6-year-old, school starts in September

Skip the allergy machinery. Draft the request citing circular 0404: substitutes in every activity with food or craft materials, including trips and birthdays, a gluten-free offering wherever food is sold, and portions in a different colour with prominent marking. Remind the parents to file the health declaration before the year starts.

### Example 3: 17-year-old with celiac worried about the army

Reassure: regular enlistment profile with a celiac clause, profile 97 absent other limitations, manoeuvring combat open on a volunteer basis. Name the specific exclusions honestly, including the David's Sling carve-out. Explain the food stipend exists on top of dining-hall provision, and give the open and closed base daily figures with the IDF page's date.

### Example 4: Family asks whether they get an allowance

Answer honestly that celiac is not a named criterion on the disabled-child impairment list, and that the section 45 tax credit therefore does not open either. Then pivot to what is real: the municipal welfare funding route via social services with a physician's recommendation and its published ceiling, the subsidised gluten-free flour, and the medication-alternative right.

## Gotchas

These are agent failure modes, not user errors.

1. **Do not advise a gluten-free diet before testing.** The defining mistake in the domain: answering "we think it's gluten" with dietary tips instead of "get tested first" harms the user.
2. **Do not tell a user to restart gluten on their own.** Once they have already stopped, reintroduction is a medical decision. Route it to the doctor.
3. **Do not import the allergy playbook.** No EpiPen, no anaphylaxis, no 101 pathway, no Ministry of Education allergen-free-zone circular. Those belong to `israeli-food-allergy-navigator`.
4. **Do not imply a Bituach Leumi allowance.** Allergy has a named criterion and celiac does not. Agents generalise from the sibling skill and invent an entitlement.
5. **Do not quote the IDF stipend without its date, or claim it is unpublished.** The IDF page states 21.9 and 34.9 NIS a day (open and closed base) as of 27.11.2022, and the amount may change. Quote it as dated, never invent another figure.
6. **Do not equate kosher-for-Passover with gluten free, or kitniyot with gluten.** Two separate vocabularies, and conflating them puts matza flour on a celiac plate.
7. **Do not treat a single negative serology as conclusive** without noting the IgA-deficiency caveat.
8. **Do not present the birthday-snack shortlist as certification.** It is a dated community list and recipes change. Say "that product is on a shortlist, check the marking on the pack", never "that product is gluten free".
9. **Do not generalise a marking from a brand to its whole range.** The beigele pair is verified; Petit Beurre and the Kinder eggs are community reports only, so check the pack.

## Troubleshooting

### Problem: "We cut out gluten a month ago and now the doctor wants tests"

Common and not a disaster, but it must be disclosed: the diet can mislead the tests. The doctor decides what happens next, including any gluten challenge. Do not design that plan.

### Problem: "The school says they cannot guarantee no cross-contamination"

Separate the two asks. Request the written entitlement (Step 3) specifically and in writing, rather than a guarantee the kitchen cannot give.

### Problem: "The pharmacy says there is no gluten-free version of the drug"

Check whether the drug actually contains wheat-derived flour or starch. If it does and the basket option is unsuitable, raise the right to a suitable alternative with the physician and the kupah.
